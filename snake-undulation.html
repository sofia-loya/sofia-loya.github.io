Snake Undulation: Development of Multi-Modal Locomotion in a Bio-Inspired System

A modular, 7-segment gliding snake robot built to test whether aerial undulation — the motion real gliding snakes use mid-flight — actually increases glide distance. // situation

The problem

The gliding snake Chrysopelea paradisi actively undulates its body while airborne to maintain stability, but whether that undulation actually increases how far it glides had never been tested in a controlled experiment. As a five-person team in a bio-inspired design course, we set out to build a physical robot that could isolate and test that one variable — comparing horizontal glide distance with and without undulation under identical launch conditions.

// task

My role

My responsibility was the motor torque analysis that determined which actuators the robot could safely use — work that fed directly into the electrical team's motor selection and the final mechanical design of the side modules. I also contributed to the experimental testing of the physical robot and the statistical analysis used to evaluate whether undulation meaningfully changed glide distance.

// action

What I did

  • Modeled each module's joint angle as a sinusoidal traveling wave and derived the peak angular acceleration each motor would need to overcome
  • Applied Newton's second law and the parallel-axis theorem to calculate the required torque at each joint, treating the center module as the origin and analyzing only half the (symmetric) robot
  • Compared four different cross-section models (thin rod, hoop, solid cylinder, hollow cylinder) to bound the moment-of-inertia estimate for each module
  • Designed and torque-tested two candidate side-module materials — PLA (1.25 g/cm³) and balsa wood (0.16 g/cm³) — and passed both torque tables to the electrical team for motor selection
  • Calculated a motor safety factor for every joint against 50% of the servo's stall torque, to keep the design well within a safe working envelope
  • Contributed to physical launch testing across four experimental conditions (1.1 m/s and 1.8 m/s, with and without undulation, 5 trials each), and to the statistical analysis — Anderson-Darling normality testing followed by two-sample t-tests — used to evaluate the results

The core calculation: each module i follows a sinusoidal joint trajectory, and the peak angular acceleration sets the worst-case torque demand at each joint.

θᵢ(t) = A · sin(ωt + φᵢ)
θ̈ᵢ(t) = −Aω² · sin(ωt + φᵢ) → peak magnitude |θ̈|max = Aω²
τⱼ = Ij,total · |θ̈|max (Newton's second law at joint j)
I = Icm + m·d² (parallel-axis theorem, d = distance from pivot to center of mass)
SFⱼ = τsafe / τⱼ, where τsafe = 0.50 · τstall = 0.0735 N·m

A = π/4 rad (max amplitude), ω = 2.8 rad/s. Four Icm models were compared — thin rod, hoop, solid cylinder, hollow cylinder — to bound the estimate. SFⱼ ≥ 1 means the motor operates safely; the innermost joint (highest inertia) was the limiting case.

// result

The outcome

Switching the side-module material from PLA to balsa wood — informed directly by my torque analysis — improved the innermost motor's safety factor from 3× to 4×, giving the team a lighter, safer final design. On the physical robot, undulation did not improve glide distance at the lower launch speed of 1.1 m/s (p = 0.998), but at 1.8 m/s it increased mean glide distance by 8.7%, from 0.595 ± 0.024 m to 0.647 ± 0.028 m (p = 0.010) — a statistically significant result showing that undulation's benefit depends on reaching a sufficient launch velocity.
